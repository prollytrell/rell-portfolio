IOC Searches Aren't Threat Hunting (and Why That Matters)

Searching for known-bad indicators is valuable work, but threat hunting begins with a hypothesis about behavior. Examples of what is, and isn't, hunting.

By Terrell Grenyion • 2025 • ~3 min read
← Back to Blog

While studying for the CySA+, I have spent more time examining what threat hunting means beyond how the term is commonly used. A conversation with my mentor helped me articulate something I had not fully put into words: much of the work labeled "threat hunting" is valuable, but it is not always hunting.

Known-bad searches versus hunting

Many teams describe threat hunting as searching historical data for known malicious indicators: connections to known command-and-control servers, malicious domains, compromised websites, or known malware files. This is important work. It validates threat intelligence and confirms that nothing obvious was missed. In each of those cases, however, we already know what we are looking for, and that is the key difference.

Threat hunting is not about confirming known malicious activity. It is about proactively searching for unknown threats by forming hypotheses and testing them against behavior in the environment.

Hunting starts with a question

Instead of asking, "Have we ever communicated with this known malicious IP?", threat hunting asks questions such as:

  • Why is an HR endpoint communicating with production or finance systems?
  • Why is an endpoint making regular outbound connections on a fixed schedule?
  • Why is a large volume of data being accessed or transferred outside business hours?
  • Why is traffic on port 443 not actually using TLS?
  • Why are domain credentials being submitted to an external site at all?

That shift from indicators to behavior is the core of real threat hunting.

Examples of threat hunting

  • Investigating abnormal account creation or privilege changes.
  • Looking for lateral movement patterns.
  • Identifying beaconing behavior regardless of destination.
  • Validating whether existing detections or controls missed activity.

Examples of what threat hunting is not

  • One-time searches for known malicious IPs or domains.
  • Verifying the presence of a known malware file.
  • Indicator-of-compromise (IOC) checks without a hypothesis.
  • Relying solely on threat intelligence feeds.

These activities remain critical; they simply fall under mature security operations rather than threat hunting.

Conclusion

What stood out most to me is that threat hunting often uncovers more than external threats. It can surface internal misuse, over-permissioned accounts, risky automation, and workflows that introduce unintended risk. In that sense, threat hunting strengthens both detection capability and overall security posture.
