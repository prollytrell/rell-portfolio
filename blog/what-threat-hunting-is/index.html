IOC Searches Aren't Threat Hunting (and Why That Matters)

Searching for known-bad indicators is valuable work, but threat hunting starts with a hypothesis about behavior. Examples of what is and isn't hunting.

By Terrell Grenyion • 2025 • ~3 min read
← Back to Blog

While studying for my CySA+, I've been spending more time learning what threat hunting really means beyond how the term is commonly used. A conversation with my mentor helped clarify something I hadn't fully articulated before: a lot of work labeled as "threat hunting" is valuable, but it isn't always hunting.

Many teams describe threat hunting as searching historical data for known bad indicators. Looking for connections to known C2 servers, malicious domains, compromised websites, or known malware files is important security work. It helps validate threat intelligence and confirm that nothing obvious was missed. But in those cases, we already know what we're looking for, and that's the difference.

Threat hunting isn't about confirming known bad activity. It's about proactively looking for unknown threats by forming hypotheses and testing them against behavior in the environment.

Instead of asking, "Have we ever talked to this known bad IP?" threat hunting asks questions such as:

  • Why is an HR endpoint communicating with production or finance systems?
  • Why is an endpoint making regular outbound connections on a fixed schedule?
  • Why is large data being accessed or transferred outside business hours?
  • Why is traffic over port 443 not actually using TLS?
  • Why are domain credentials being submitted to an external site at all?

That shift from indicators to behavior is the core of real threat hunting.

Examples of threat hunting

  • Investigating abnormal account creation or privilege changes.
  • Looking for lateral movement patterns.
  • Identifying beaconing behavior regardless of destination.
  • Validating whether existing detections or controls missed activity.

Examples of what threat hunting is not

  • One-time searches for known bad IPs or domains.
  • Verifying the presence of a known malware file.
  • IOC checks without a hypothesis.
  • Relying solely on threat intelligence lists.

These activities are still critical. They just fall under mature security operations rather than threat hunting.

What really stood out to me is that threat hunting often uncovers more than external threats. It can surface internal misuse, over-permissioned accounts, risky automation, or workflows that introduce unintended risk. In that sense, threat hunting strengthens both detection capabilities and overall security posture.
