An AI Use Policy Should Guide Usage, Not Just Restrict It

What I learned drafting an AI use policy at work: enabling good use, data rules by data type, and human accountability matter more than a list of banned tools.

By Terrell Grenyion • 2026 • ~2 min read
← Back to Blog

Within the past year or so, I had the opportunity to draft an AI use policy for work, and I'll admit my thinking going into it was pretty narrow compared to now. I assumed it would mostly be a list of rules. Which tools people are allowed to use, which ones they aren't, and making sure nobody pastes sensitive data into a prompt.

Those things are still important. However, the more time I spent on it, the more I realized that a policy built mostly on restrictions doesn't really help anyone do their job. People are going to use AI either way, so the goal shouldn't just be telling them what not to do. It should be showing them how to use it well.

For a policy and/or training aimed at people using AI in their day-to-day work, these are the areas I now think matter most:

  • Enabling good use, meaning people know which tools are approved, what they're useful for, and how to actually get value out of them.
  • Data rules based on the type of data, so instead of a vague "don't share anything sensitive," people know exactly what can go into which tools.
  • Human accountability, so whatever AI produces, a person is still reviewing it and owning the final result, especially if it's going to a client or being used to make a decision.

If I were looking at it from a full business perspective, I would add vendor and access risk to that list as well. Things like where the data ends up, how long it's retained, who has access to API keys, and how usage costs are controlled. For the people actually using these tools, though, those three are where I would start.

Looking back, the biggest shift for me was moving from thinking about AI policy as a way to control usage to thinking about it as a way to guide it.
