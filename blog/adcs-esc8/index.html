AD CS ESC8: What Defenders Should Look For

After a workplace penetration test, I researched Active Directory Certificate Services relay attacks (ESC8) and wrote up the log sources, indicators, and investigation questions defenders can use.

By Terrell Grenyion • 2026 • ~3 min read
← Back to Blog

Recently, I was tasked with an assignment that required me to research and understand ESC8 attacks and how they're conducted. Going through this was a good reminder of how subtle some attack paths can be, especially when they abuse legitimate infrastructure instead of relying on obvious malware.

At a high level, ESC8 attacks abuse Active Directory Certificate Services (AD CS) by leveraging misconfigurations that allow an attacker to obtain certificate-based authentication material they shouldn't have access to. Instead of exploiting a vulnerability in the traditional sense, the attacker abuses trust.

ESC8 can be conducted in a few different ways. In some cases, an attacker is able to request a certificate on behalf of a privileged account due to overly permissive template settings. In others, the attack relies on coercing authentication from a machine account and relaying that authentication to the certificate authority to obtain a usable certificate.

This attack emphasizes how much visibility and context matter. Because certificates are a legitimate authentication mechanism, the resulting access often looks clean. That's what makes this class of attacks particularly dangerous, and you must look beyond traditional indicators.

Indicators worth correlating

Some of the indicators that become more meaningful when viewed together:

  • Unusual certificate requests.
  • Certificate enrollments tied to accounts that don't normally request them.
  • Authentication activity using certificates where passwords are typically used.
  • Requests originating from unexpected systems or network segments.

These matter most when any of them are followed by lateral movement or privileged access shortly afterward.

Defensive takeaways

This task reinforced an important lesson for me in that detection isn't just about alerts. It's about understanding normal behavior well enough to recognize when something doesn't belong. From a defensive standpoint, the biggest takeaway was the importance of reviewing AD CS configurations regularly. Understanding which certificate templates exist, why they exist, and who can enroll in them matters a lot. Visibility into certificate issuance, enrollment failures, and authentication events tied to certificates is critical.

Questions to ask during an investigation

Good detection also comes from asking better questions:

  • Which systems normally talk to the certificate authority?
  • Which accounts typically use certificate-based authentication?
  • What does normal issuance volume look like?

Without that baseline, it's difficult to spot abuse when it happens, especially in environments that rely heavily on certificates.

Working through this research helped me better understand how certificate abuse actually shows up in practice. It's one thing to know that certificate abuse exists. It's another to understand how it would actually appear in logs, what questions to ask during an investigation, and why these attacks can be so difficult to detect.
