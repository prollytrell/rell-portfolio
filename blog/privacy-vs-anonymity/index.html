Privacy Is Not Anonymity: What VPNs and Incognito Mode Actually Do

VPNs, private browsing, and hardened browsers each solve a different problem. Most people need privacy rather than anonymity, and common tools don't always deliver what users assume.

By Terrell Grenyion • 2026 • ~3 min read
← Back to Blog

One topic I see misunderstood often is the difference between privacy and anonymity online. The terms are frequently used interchangeably, but they are not the same, and the tools people rely on do not always provide what they expect.

Defining the terms

Privacy is about controlling who can see your data and how it is used. Anonymity is about concealing who you are. You can have one without the other, and in most everyday browsing, people are seeking privacy rather than true anonymity. VPNs, private browsing modes, and hardened browsers each address part of that goal.

VPNs

A VPN helps protect your traffic from observation by your internet service provider or by others on the same network, which is especially valuable on public Wi-Fi. It does not make you anonymous. Websites can still identify you through cookies, browser fingerprinting, the accounts you sign into, and behavioral patterns.

Private browsing

Private or incognito mode is another commonly misunderstood feature. It primarily prevents your browser from saving history, cookies, and form data locally. It does not stop websites, advertisers, or network operators from seeing what you do while you are online.

Hardened browsers

The browser itself plays a significant role. Default configurations are usually optimized for convenience and compatibility, not maximum privacy. I have been experimenting with Betterfox, a hardened Firefox configuration that tightens privacy, security, and performance settings. What I value most is its transparency: rather than relying on a black-box product, you deliberately choose what is enabled or disabled. Arkenfox is another widely praised option.

What better privacy looks like

For most people, better privacy means:

  • Understanding what your tools actually do.
  • Reducing unnecessary tracking and data collection.
  • Hardening your browser where possible.
  • Being intentional about which accounts you sign into.

Conclusion

True anonymity requires a very different threat model and a different set of tools, and most users do not need it for daily browsing. What they do need is a clear understanding of what their current tools protect, and what they do not.
