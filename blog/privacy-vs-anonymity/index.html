Privacy Is Not Anonymity: What VPNs and Incognito Mode Actually Do

VPNs, private browsing, and hardened browsers each solve a different problem. Most people need privacy, not anonymity, and the tools don't always deliver what they assume.

By Terrell Grenyion • 2026 • ~3 min read
← Back to Blog

One topic I see misunderstood a lot is the difference between privacy and anonymity online. They're often used interchangeably, but they're not the same thing, and the tools people rely on don't always provide what they think they do.

Privacy is about controlling who can see your data and how it's used. Anonymity is about hiding who you are. You can have one without the other, and in most everyday browsing scenarios, people are usually aiming for privacy, not true anonymity.

This is where things like VPNs, private browsing modes, and hardened browsers come into play.

VPNs

A VPN helps protect your traffic from being easily observed by your ISP or someone on the same network, especially on public Wi-Fi. It does not make you anonymous. Websites can still identify you through cookies, browser fingerprints, accounts you log into, and behavioral patterns.

Private browsing

Private or incognito mode is another commonly misunderstood feature. It mainly prevents your browser from saving history, cookies, and form data locally. It doesn't stop websites, advertisers, or networks from seeing what you're doing while you're online.

Hardened browsers

Browsers themselves play a huge role here. Default browser configurations are often optimized for convenience and compatibility, not maximum privacy. I recently decided to experiment with Betterfox, a hardened Firefox configuration that focuses on tightening privacy, security, and performance controls. What I like about it is the transparency. You're not relying on a black-box tool; you're intentionally choosing what gets enabled or disabled. Arkenfox is another I see frequently that is highly praised.

What better privacy looks like

For most people, better privacy looks like:

  • Understanding what your tools actually do.
  • Reducing unnecessary tracking and data collection.
  • Hardening your browser where possible.
  • Being intentional about what accounts you log into.

True anonymity requires a very different threat model and different tools altogether, and most users don't actually need it for daily browsing.
